## What this video is actually about
A new AI consultant closes their first client, gets excited, sends the invoice, the money hits, and the very next thing they do is open their automation tool and start building — based on how the client explained the process on the sales call. Three weeks later they're stressed, the client is frustrated, and they're working at midnight because things don't connect. The speaker's premise: most first projects don't fall apart because of bad automation; they fall apart before a single line is written in Claude Code, because of assumptions made on the sales call. He walks through five things to lock in before building anything so the project actually runs as supposed to. He also pitches his own funnel: a 4-week challenge inside his free School community, with a free course and direct one-on-one access to him (link below).

## Key lessons
Five things to lock in before building anything:

1. Get a live process walk-through. Have the client walk through the actual process live, step by step, screen share — not a summary, not a Loom they recorded last month. Ask: a lead comes in, where does it land? Who sees it first? What do they do with it? And then what? You'll find steps that never came up on the sales call, day-of-week dependencies, forgotten tools, and load-bearing spreadsheets or Slack channels. Builds break in the assumptions, not the automation logic. The speaker's own example: he built an email automation for an insulation company thinking he knew the workflow, sent a campaign to 200 insulation companies, and it failed across the board because of background manual work he had no visibility into — costing him 200 prospects.

2. Make the client define "done." Ask one question and then stop talking: "The project is complete when blank." Let them answer; don't fill the silence or help them along. Write it down word for word, then send it back in writing (email): "hey just confirming what we discussed. The project is complete when X, Y, and Z. Sound right?" Anything outside that is a separate project with a separate scope and probably a separate invoice.

3. Get full admin access to every tool on day one. Right after they sign, before kickoff, list every tool in the workflow (CRM, email platform, calendar, website, payment processor, phone system, everything connected) and ask if they have full admin access — not partial, not "kind of." The speaker uses a Google Doc checklist of common tools, the access level needed, and a checkbox per item; find what's missing while the project hasn't started, not 3 weeks in. Examples he's seen: missing login, account set up 5 years ago by a departed employee, previous agency owns the domain, someone's husband's cousin set up the CRM and alone holds the API keys.

4. Map what happens after the automation runs. Ask "What happens after my automation runs?" Map the whole chain, not just your part — what does someone do with the output, who's responsible, when are they checking it? Examples: an AI voice agent books appointments on the owner's calendar but nobody told the owner to check the calendar in the morning, so leads get booked and nobody takes the call; a confirmation email gets a reply that nobody checks for 2 days and the lead goes cold. The system worked perfectly but the client thinks it's broken. If there's an ownership gap, flag it — fix inside scope or tell the client clearly: "my system stops here. For this to work end-to-end, here's what someone on your team needs to be doing."

5. Plant the seed for the next engagement near the end of the build (while everything's working and the client's happy). Casually say: "now that this is running, the next thing that I see most businesses like yours look at is X. Is that something you'd want to explore later?" Don't pitch, don't push. A huge percentage say yes because they trust you. The next project is lined up before this one closes — no proposal, no discovery call. This avoids the hamster wheel of cold outreach and being stuck at "$1,500 projects forever"; one good project can turn into two or three, and eventually a recurring retainer.

Overarching rule: don't open Claude Code / don't start building until all five are locked in.
