## What this video is actually about
Nick Saraev's "start over" step-by-step playbook for launching an AI consulting business in 2026 from $0 to $25,000/month or beyond. It walks the five pillars of an AI consulting agency in the order a lead flows through the business: marketing, sales, onboarding, fulfillment, and retention. The creator promises free templates, scripts, and workflows via a link in the description, and pitches his own products at the end (Maker School community, Gumroad template library, Leftclick consulting firm). Creator states he scaled multiple agencies to $160,000/month combined revenue and spent hundreds of thousands of his own money mapping the route — self-stated, unverified.

## Key lessons
- At $0 revenue, marketing is the only thing that matters; spending time on anything not directly generating leads is wasting money.
- Start with platform sales (Upwork/Freelancer/Fiverr) because it puts you in front of business owners who already know their problem and their budget — the "top of the buying ladder" — so you skip problem/solution education.
- Four lead-gen mechanisms ranked on time, capital, and technical barrier: platform sales (high time, low capital, low barrier, least scalable), cold email (low time, medium upfront cost, very high barrier, highest upside), communities (high time, very low cost, low barrier, mid-tier results), cold DMs (high time, low capital, very high barrier).
- Sales = uncover pain → show how your solution solves it → position yourself as the obvious investment. Never open with price.
- Discovery is the most important part of the sales call; asking "why did you book this call?" gets you ~70–80% of the way to closing.
- Onboarding should be frontloaded into a ~30-minute kickoff call where platform access/2FA is handled live, to avoid weeks of email chase.
- Fulfillment has two parts: communication (managing the client's perception of progress) and delivery (packaging the system like a product). Daily 60-second check-ins build trust and can let you double prices.
- Retention: overdeliver by ~5% right after delivery (fix something extra + documentation) to trigger reciprocity, then pitch a follow-up project or managed service while the client is most excited and trusting.

## Acquisition
Four marketing mechanisms (in lead-flow order):
1. Platform sales — Upwork, Freelancer.com, Fiverr. Recommended starting point. CV must pop; custom videos/assets to make proposals stand out; templates in description. Creator claims well over $500,000 directly from Upwork.
2. Cold email — low time (initial setup + small ongoing), medium upfront cost, very high barrier to entry, highest upside; scales independent of time (20 vs 2,000 emails/day with the same system). Creator claims he helped generate millions with cold email, more than with Upwork. High-performing formula + scripts in description. First campaign will probably be your worst; needs runway and perseverance.
3. Communities — Slack, School, Reddit (Maker School named). High time outlay, very low cost, low barrier, mid-tier results. Objective: build authority and become the go-to person; show up every day or two, comment/post value solving community questions (filter community by questions), DM people who ask questions; post templates in description.
4. Cold DMs — Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, X (all free). High time, low capital, very high barrier (psychology techniques). Copy-paste templates get ghosted; messages must seem precise and hyperpersonalized yet informal (not no-effort). Structure from Instantly (cold email platform): personalization → value prop → niche → goal → relevant case study → cliffhanger → CTA. Example pitch and script in description. ## Sales
Six-step sales structure (~30–45 minutes): rapport → schedule → discovery → solutions → investments → proposal. Creator claims he taught it to 6,000+ people, with students closing deals worth as much as $60,000 upfront.
- Rapport: get them to smile in ~10 seconds (light joke, compliment, or "Hey, where are you based?").
- Schedule: frame the call — why they booked → dig into the business and numbers → if fit, show what it looks like and talk next steps; get permission ("Does that sound good?").
- Discovery: most important part; ask the questions you want answers to, the client gives you the ammunition. Key questions: "Why did you decide to book the call?" "What about my offer made you want to say yes?" "What is the goal right now?" "Assuming we fix this for you, what are you thinking it'll do to the company?" "Why haven't you guys done this yet?" Plus follow-ups (e.g., "how much is a client paying when they get in the door?").
- Solutions: position your system as the logical solution to the pain ("we've solved many similar problems… we're going to solve X by doing Y… my hope is it will save you Z dollars every month… would you like to see an example?"), then demo from a library of 5–10 templated systems. Productized/templated sales: duplicate and spend ~15 minutes touching up at delivery.
- Investment: drop the price at the end after value is built. Framing example: "based on what you told me to save you that $50,000 price tag, our project would cost 19 grand. assuming there's nothing else we need to add on, your investment would pay for itself in literally the first month." Frame as investment, then shut up ("first person to speak loses"). If they want cheaper, remove line items/functionality — don't discount. "I need to think about it" is almost never the real reason; go one level deeper ("would this system solve the needs you put in front of me?"). Don't force a decision on the call — consultants aren't car sales, margins aren't 3%. Send a proposal and set it to automatically follow up.
- Proposal: written scope (technical work; expectation mismatch → bloated scopes). Platforms named: Panda (likely PandaDoc) and Better Proposals, with automatic follow-up and time-expiry offers. Don't close directly on the call; clients prefer logical + emotional decisions for technical work.

## Fulfillment
Two parts: communication + delivery.
- Communication: manage the perception of the engagement — if the client doesn't feel progress, they assume none. Simple templates, not novels. Daily check-in Mon–Fri over Slack/email/WhatsApp (~60 seconds per client): "Hey client, just checking in. No action needed from your end. We just finished XYZ thing. We just started XYZ thing. Uh, let you know how things are going tomorrow. Thanks for the time." Builds massive trust; can double lifetime value and let you double prices; differentiator is perception management, not the service itself.
- Delivery: package systems like products (diamond analogy: box, satin, bow, card) — don't hand over raw make.com access. Examples: automated invoicing system, LinkedIn scraper, Slack workflow, make.com scenario. Record a 10–15 minute Loom video walkthrough + documentation. Gumroad library of templates/builds/automations the creator personally built and sold to clients for anything from $2,000 to $15,000.

## Exact tactics
- Upwork: pop the CV, attach custom videos/assets, use proposal templates to get ~80% of the way there.
- Cold email: use the formula/scripts from the description; expect the first campaign to be the worst; persist with runway.
- Communities: filter the community by questions, build a content calendar solving one question per day/week, be a "reply guy," DM people who ask questions.
- Cold DMs: apply the Instantly structure; be informal but visibly effortful; use the example pitch ("Hey Sarah…") adapted to niche.
- Sales: open with "where are you based?"; frame the call before discovery; run discovery questions; demo from a 5–10 system library; quote price at the end then stay silent; de-scope instead of discounting; probe "think about it" with one deeper question; send a scoped proposal with auto follow-ups and optional time expiry; don't close on the call.
- Onboarding: ~30-minute kickoff call with introduction, goals/timelines (example: start today, wrap within 3 weeks, three milestones), communication expectations (one daily update Mon–Fri), handle platform access and 2FA live on the call, client signs up to platforms themselves to avoid handoffs, close by confirming the first update tomorrow.
- Fulfillment: send the daily check-in template every weekday; record a Loom walkthrough + documentation after delivery; package with a "diamond" wrap (email dressing it up).
- Retention: overdeliver by an additional 5% (fix something found during the build + documentation), then pitch a follow-up project or managed service (e.g., funnel SMS optimization) while reciprocity is high.

## Mistakes & warnings
- At $0, working on anything but lead generation is wasting money; quit procrastinating.
- Cold email's first campaign will probably be your worst; it has a very high barrier to entry (deep copy/infrastructure skills) and requires runway and perseverance.
- Communities are a grind and not very scalable; mid-tier results.
- Cold DMs with copy-paste templates get ghosted more often than not; results vary wildly by niche and offer.
- Opening with price makes the client fixate on a number and spend the call on justification.
- Discounting when asked for cheaper reduces value; remove scope/line items instead.
- "I need to think about it" is almost never the real reason — there is almost certainly another objection.
- Don't push clients to decide on the call; consultants aren't car sales (margins aren't 3%).
- Letting clients swallow disproportionate time with silly questions/random calls is a beginner trap; set firm boundaries.
- Fulfillment is not just delivering the system — hand over raw make.com access and you haven't productized.
- Disappearing after a great project leaves money on the table ("hitting a home run and then forgetting to actually run the bases").
